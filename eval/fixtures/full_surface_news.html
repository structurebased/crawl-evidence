Cascadia Metering breach exposed 2.1 million utility accounts

By Elena Vasquez and Tom Adeyemi. Published October 21, 2026 at 6:15 a.m. PDT. Updated October 22, 2026 at 11:40 a.m. PDT.

Attackers who breached the metering contractor Cascadia Metering spent at least twenty-nine days reading customer records for 2.1 million utility accounts before a contractor in Spokane noticed an authentication log that did not match the shift schedule.

The intrusion, described in a draft report this newsroom obtained from a state regulator, reached names, service addresses, account numbers and eleven years of interval consumption data. Payment card numbers and bank details were not stored in the compromised system, according to the report and to two people with direct knowledge of the investigation.

Fourteen water and electric districts in three states contracted with Cascadia to run the back-end database behind their advanced metering infrastructure. The company, based in Bend, Oregon, employs 340 people and has not previously disclosed a security incident.

The breach is the largest known compromise of a metering contractor in the United States and the third incident since 2024 to expose detailed energy use patterns for households, data that researchers have shown can reveal when a home is occupied, what medical devices it runs and whether a resident has installed solar panels.

What happened

SENTINEL-NEWS-TEXT. According to the draft report, the attackers entered through a virtual private network account that belonged to a Cascadia employee who left the company in February. The account was never disabled. It carried a password that had not been rotated in nineteen months and was protected only by a single factor, because the contractor that administered the VPN had exempted the account from multi-factor enforcement during a migration.

Once inside, the attackers moved laterally over four days to a database server that held the interval data. They did not use malware. Instead they ran ordinary SQL queries through a read-only reporting tool that every district dispatcher uses to answer customer calls, the report says. The tool logged queries, but the log rotated after seventy-two hours.

The compromised concentrators

Separately, the report describes a second, smaller exposure involving 6,400 cellular concentrators, the cabinets that collect readings from neighbourhoods of meters and forward them to Cascadia. Attackers could not read household data through the concentrators, but they could restart them, and for eleven hours on September 30 a swath of eastern Washington reported no readings at all.

District officials attributed the gap to a firmware push and did not connect it to the intrusion until October 14, when Cascadia told them customer records had been accessed. The company has since replaced credentials on all 6,400 cabinets and moved their management interface behind a separate jump host.

How the intrusion unfolded

The timeline below is reconstructed from the draft report, from log excerpts reviewed by this newsroom and from interviews with nine people involved in the response, most of whom spoke on condition of anonymity because they were not authorised to discuss an active investigation.

Twenty-six minutes of silence

The longest unexplained gap in the logs falls on October 2, between 2:14 and 2:40 a.m. Pacific. Automated queries resume at 2:40 with a burst of 1,900 requests in ninety seconds, roughly forty times the normal overnight rate. A monitoring rule that should have paged the on-call engineer was disabled while Cascadia migrated its alerting stack, the report states.

By the time the alerting rule returned on October 5, the attackers had already copied the bulk of the dataset to a staging server inside Cascadia's own network. They transferred it out in eleven batches over the following week, each batch shaped to stay under the network monitoring tool's per-flow volume threshold.

What customers should do

Districts began mailing notices on October 19 and most have posted a dedicated page with a call centre number. Consumers do not need to change anything about their service, and Cascadia has said it will not charge for credit monitoring.

The practical risk is not identity theft in the usual sense. Interval data is valuable to burglars, to insurers and to anyone building a model of a household's daily routine. Security researchers who have studied similar breaches recommend checking whether your district's public portal exposes hourly usage to anyone with an account number, because account numbers were among the fields taken.

A configuration that would have helped

Two independent reviewers of the draft report pointed to the same omission: Cascadia's reporting database accepted queries from any host on the internal network, with no source allow-list. A three-line rule on the database proxy would have confined access to the dispatch subnet and would have blocked the lateral move entirely, they said. Cascadia declined to comment on the configuration.

The regulator's response

The Oregon Public Utility Commission opened an inquiry on October 15 and has subpoenaed Cascadia's access logs and its contract with each district. Washington's utilities and transportation commission said it is reviewing whether Cascadia met the cybersecurity baseline the state adopted in 2023 for metering vendors.

Commissioner Ruth Achebe, in a written statement, called the incident "a failure of basic account hygiene" and said the inquiry would examine whether districts audited their contractor's offboarding process. Idaho has no equivalent baseline; two of its affected districts said they relied on Cascadia's annual SOC 2 report, which covered the period before the VPN account was created.

Earlier findings at the district

Harbor County PUD's own inspector general flagged missing multi-factor enforcement on vendor VPN accounts in a March 2025 memo, according to a copy reviewed by this newsroom. The memo recommended a quarterly review of dormant accounts. A PUD spokesperson said the recommendation was accepted but that the first review was scheduled for January 2027.

Inland Power Cooperative told members in a letter that it had asked Cascadia twice this year for a penetration test summary and had received a letter of attestation instead. The cooperative's general manager said the letter did not specify which controls were tested.

How the districts responded

Cascadia Metering contracts with fourteen water and electric districts in Oregon, Washington and Idaho. We asked each one when it learned of the intrusion, what it told customers, and whether it has since moved its data off Cascadia's systems. Six answered in writing, two declined, and six did not respond to three calls and two emails each.

Six answers on the record

Bend Electric, Cascadia's largest client at 412,000 accounts, said it learned of the intrusion on October 14 and notified customers on October 18 by email and door hanger. The utility has asked Cascadia for a copy of the draft report and has not received it.

Spokane River Utility said it found the breach itself on October 2, when its own monitoring flagged a 1,900-query burst against the reporting database. The utility told Cascadia the same day. Cascadia did not confirm a security incident for another twelve days.

Rogue Valley Water kept a local nightly extract of meter reads and restored billing from its own copy on October 6. The district printed shutoff notices by hand for a week and fielded 211 calls from customers asking why their usage graph was blank.

Yakima Valley Power has not notified customers. The utility says it is waiting for direction from the state commission, a position three state representatives challenged in a letter to the Washington Utilities and Transportation Commission on October 24.

Klamath County Water learned of the intrusion on October 15 and mailed letters to 3,100 affected accounts the following Monday, offering a year of credit monitoring through a vendor Cascadia selected.

Wenatchee Public Utilities has terminated its Cascadia contract effective December 31 and issued a request for proposals for a replacement vendor on October 28. The district's general manager, Ruth Calloway, said the decision was about notification, not the breach itself.

What we still do not know

The draft report answers how the attackers got in. It does not answer what they took, who else is exposed, or why a departed employee's VPN account was still live eleven months after the separation.

Open questions

Whether interval consumption data was copied. The report documents eleven outbound transfers between October 5 and October 13, each under 4 gigabytes. It does not say what was in them, because the staging server the data landed on was wiped before forensics reached it.

How many of the 2.1 million accounts were actually opened. Cascadia logs authentication, not row reads. The report says application log retention was ninety days, and the intrusion began forty-five days before discovery.

Whether the departed employee's account appeared on a leaver review. Cascadia's own 2025 security audit listed stale VPN accounts as a finding. The audit summary Cascadia shared with districts redacts the affected account count and the remediation deadline.

Whether other Cascadia clients are affected. The company lists twenty-six municipal clients on its website. The commission's notice went to the fourteen districts in its jurisdiction; four of the remaining twelve are in states with no equivalent reporting rule.

Whether the state will fine Cascadia. Oregon's 2024 statute allows up to $25,000 per day for a reporting violation. Washington's rule has no fine schedule, only a duty to report within seventy-two hours, which Cascadia missed by eleven days.

Whether Cascadia carried cyber insurance and whether the insurer has appointed panel counsel. The company's parent disclosed a $15 million cyber policy in a 2025 regulatory filing; the policy's exclusions are not public.

Whether the restart of 6,400 concentrators on September 30 was coincidence or an attempt to age out device logs. The report calls the timing unexplained and says Cascadia has not provided the change ticket.

What customers should do now

Customers of the fourteen districts have asked us the same six questions. Here are the answers, with the authority for each.

Questions from customers

Should I put a freeze on my credit?
Not on the basis of this incident alone. The exposed data categories are names, service addresses, account numbers and interval reads. No payment card or bank details were stored in the compromised system. A freeze is free and reversible, so you may do it for peace of mind, but it is not a documented response to what is known so far.
Can my usage data reveal when I am home?
Yes. Interval reads taken every fifteen minutes can identify occupancy patterns, the presence of medical equipment and, in a 2024 field study of 2,100 homes, the approximate start and end of sleep. Eleven years of that data for 2.1 million accounts is the part of this breach that researchers say matters most.
Why was my bill fine if the data was stolen?
Billing reads come from the same database, but the intrusion targeted the reporting archive, not the billing pipeline. Districts that kept their own nightly extracts billed normally throughout. Your bill is not evidence that your records were untouched.
Can I stop interval data collection?
In Oregon and Washington, yes, with a paper-read request filed at the district. Idaho has no equivalent right. Turning off interval reads trades the exposure above for a manual meter route, and the district must still store your account record.
Who pays for new smart meters or locks?
Nobody has offered to. Meter replacement is not a documented mitigation here, because the meters were not compromised, only the database behind them. Two districts have offered to reissue account numbers on request; ask your district's customer service line.
Can customers sue Cascadia?
Three firms have filed notices of intent in state court. Whether a customer has standing depends on the district contract, which typically assigns claims to the district. Ask for the indemnification clause in your utility's agreement; Bend Electric's is page 31.

Reporter's notebook

The first tip came in as an email on October 16 from someone who wrote only that "Cascadia lost a database and told no one." I spent two days calling district customer service lines, where the front desk had not heard of any incident, before a billing supervisor in Bend confirmed the October 14 letter.

The public records requests took between four days and five weeks. Bend Electric produced its contract with a $240 copying fee, waived after we pointed out the state's fee schedule. Harbor County released the inspector general memo the same afternoon. Yakima has not responded at all.

The hardest number to verify was the account count, because districts report meters, premises and customer accounts differently. I used the 2.1 million figure from the draft report, which counts unique customer accounts across the fourteen districts, not meters or service points.
