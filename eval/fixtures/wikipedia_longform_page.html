SENTINEL-ATLAS-TEXT Desalination is the process of removing minerals from saline water. More generally, desalination refers to the removal of salts and minerals from a target substance, for example soil. Seawater desalination is the process applied to seawater, and brackish water desalination to brackish water. The most common desalination processes are reverse osmosis and thermal distillation.

Desalination is used on ships and submarines, and in regions where freshwater is scarce or contaminated. It produces drinking water from seawater and wastewater, and can supply water for irrigation in water-scarce regions. The two main desalination methods are thermal distillation, which involves heating the water until it evaporates and then condensing the vapour, and membrane separation, which uses pressure to force water through a selective barrier. Plant quality is judged against national drinking-water guidelines.

The energy cost of desalination has fallen sharply since the 1970s, driven by improvements in membrane materials and energy recovery devices. However, desalination remains more expensive than most conventional freshwater sources, and it has environmental impacts associated with energy consumption, intake of aquatic organisms and disposal of concentrated brine.

History

Evidence that seawater was boiled for drinking aboard ships dates to the sixteenth century, when sailors on Atlantic crossings set copper stills over the galley fire. The first recorded land installation was built on Curaçao in 1828 to supply the garrison at Willemstad, using a coal-fired still that produced roughly 2,500 litres a day. For most of the nineteenth century desalination remained a military and naval concern, because only a government could absorb the fuel cost of evaporating water one litre at a time.

The Arab desalination historian Takashi Asano traces the industrial turn to the oil boom of the 1960s, when Gulf states could afford to burn crude to make freshwater and had no alternative aquifer to drill. Multi-stage flash plants at Shuwaikh in Kuwait and Jubail in Saudi Arabia scaled output from thousands to hundreds of thousands of cubic metres a day within a decade. The same period produced the first large reverse osmosis membrane trials in California, though those membranes fouled so quickly that thermal distillation kept the market until the mid-1980s.

Cost fell by two orders of magnitude between 1970 and 2010. The driving change was not a better still but a thinner membrane: thin-film composite sheets cut the energy needed to push water through a barrier from about 10 kWh per cubic metre to under 4. Today the cheapest large plants quote water at 0.50 US dollars per cubic metre before conveyance, a figure that would have been absurd to a nineteenth-century shipwright.

Processes

Thermal distillation

Thermal methods boil seawater and condense the vapour. The simplest form is single-effect distillation, which wastes most of the latent heat in the steam. Multi-stage flash improves on this by passing hot brine through a series of chambers held at progressively lower pressure, so a fraction flashes into vapour at each stage and the heat from one stage preheats the next. Multi-effect distillation arranges the same idea vertically: steam from one effect becomes the heat source for the next, and a thermal vapour compressor can recycle part of the last effect's vapour back to the first.

Thermal plants tolerate seawater with high fouling potential, which is why they persist in the Arabian Gulf where summer temperatures push salinity above 45 parts per thousand. Their drawback is energy: even with heat recovery a modern multi-stage flash plant needs the equivalent of 250 to 330 megajoules of thermal energy and about 3 kWh of electricity per cubic metre of product. Where waste heat is free, as at a co-located power station, that equation changes.

Membrane separation

Reverse osmosis forces seawater against a semipermeable membrane at pressures of 55 to 80 bar, well above the osmotic pressure of roughly 27 bar for standard seawater. Water molecules pass through the polyamide active layer while dissolved salts are swept away in the concentrate stream. Modern spiral-wound elements pack thousands of square metres of membrane into a pressure vessel the size of a domestic water heater, and a large plant strings a hundred vessels into trains.

Feed pretreatment decides whether a reverse osmosis plant meets its warranty. Suspended solids are removed by coagulation and ultrafiltration, biological growth is controlled with chlorine that must then be dechlorinated before it attacks the membrane, and antiscalant dosing keeps calcium carbonate and silica from crystallising on the surface. Energy recovery devices, typically pressure exchangers that transfer pressure from the reject stream to the incoming feed, cut specific consumption to 2.5 to 3.5 kWh per cubic metre.

Forward osmosis and membrane distillation are the two experimental branches most often cited in the literature. Forward osmosis uses a concentrated draw solution to pull water across the membrane without high pressure, then separates water from the draw solution in a second step whose energy cost is still disputed. Membrane distillation runs hot feed against a hydrophobic membrane so only vapour crosses the gap; it runs at atmospheric pressure and can use solar heat, but flux per square metre remains too low for municipal scale.

Electrodialysis and freezing

Electrodialysis moves ions rather than water: an electric field pulls dissolved salts through alternating cation- and anion-exchange membranes, leaving a diluate stream behind. It is efficient for brackish water below about 5,000 mg/L of total dissolved solids and loses its advantage on seawater, where the salt load makes current costs dominate. Electrodialysis reversal, which flips the field periodically to shed the fouling layer, is the standard commercial variant.

Freezing desalination exploits the fact that ice formed from seawater excludes most of its salt. The practical difficulty is that freezing removes less energy per kilogram than evaporation but transfers it far more slowly, so crystalliser design and brine entrainment have kept the method out of commercial service for more than a century of attempts.

Energy and cost

The thermodynamic minimum work of separating seawater at 35,000 mg/L into pure water and brine is about 1.06 kWh per cubic metre. Real plants sit two to four times above that floor, and the gap is where engineering lives. Reverse osmosis has closed the gap faster than any thermal alternative, which is why it now accounts for roughly 69 percent of global contracted desalination capacity.

Electricity price shapes plant design more than membrane price does. A plant paying 0.03 US dollars per kilowatt-hour can profitably run at high recovery and accept a larger brine stream; the same plant at 0.15 per kilowatt-hour will trade recovery for pressure-exchanger efficiency and may add photovoltaic capacity to shave the daytime peak. Saudi Arabia's Sakaka plant and the Al Khafji solar hybrid in the kingdom were built around that arithmetic.

Intake and outfall works are a second, often underestimated cost line. Beach wells avoid much of the pretreatment burden but yield less water; open intakes need screen designs that do not entrain larvae, and the diffuser that returns concentrate must be long enough to reach a mixing zone where salinity returns to background within regulatory distance. For a 100,000 cubic metre per day plant those civil works can approach a third of capital cost.

Environmental effects

The concentrate stream is the central environmental question. It carries the salt that was in the feed, plus pretreatment chemicals, antiscalants and any metals picked up in the plant. Discharged at surface level it can form a dense plume that hugs the seabed; most modern outfalls use multiport diffusers to induce rapid dilution, and modelling studies typically show salinity elevation falling below 1 part per thousand within a few hundred metres.

Intake impingement and entrainment remain the more visible harm. Fish eggs and larvae drawn through screens have near-total mortality, which is why subsurface intakes and low-velocity screen designs are now required in several jurisdictions. California's Ocean Plan amendments set entrainment performance standards that effectively favour beach wells for new plants on the coast.

Brine can also carry copper and nickel from antiscalants and corrosion, and temperature if the plant is thermal. Monitoring programmes in the Red Sea and the Gulf have documented local changes in benthic foraminifera communities near outfalls, while larger studies find recovery at distances of one to two kilometres. The honest summary is that impact is real, spatially bounded and manageable with siting, but not zero.

A less discussed effect is the carbon footprint of the energy supply. Reverse osmosis on a coal-heavy grid can emit more than 2 kilograms of carbon dioxide per cubic metre of water, which offsets the conservation benefit in water-stressed regions that also suffer heat stress. Coupling plants to renewable generation or waste heat is therefore an environmental measure, not only a cost measure.

Geographic distribution

About half of installed capacity sits in the Middle East and North Africa, where groundwater depletion, flat terrain and oil revenue combine to make desalination the default supply option for coastal cities. Spain is the largest European operator, with the Canary Islands and the south-east coast depending on reverse osmosis for between 15 and 40 percent of municipal supply. The United States is concentrated in Florida, Texas and California, where brackish groundwater desalination now outpaces seawater in new permits.

Small island states use desalination as insurance against drought rather than as a base-load source. Plants on Malta, Barbados and Singapore run at high utilisation, and Singapore's hybrid system couples reverse osmosis with reclaimed water and rainwater capture so that no single source can fail the city. That portfolio logic, rather than any single technology, is the pattern most often copied.

Social and political context

Desalination is often framed as a technical fix, but access is allocated politically. In several coastal cities piped desalinated water reaches new developments while older neighbourhoods remain on intermittent tankers, because the distribution network, not the plant, is the binding constraint. Planners who fund a plant without funding the pipes buy water that some residents will never see.

Public perception tracks trust in the operator more than trust in the chemistry. Communities near proposed outfalls have organised successfully around construction traffic, brine plume uncertainty and the visual presence of an industrial waterfront. Early, legible monitoring data and a published discharge licence reduce opposition more reliably than assurances about membrane purity.

Water pricing is the third political layer. Underpriced desalinated water encourages outdoor use and defeats the conservation purpose; priced too high it becomes a regressive charge on households that already ration. Several utilities split the bill into a volumetric rate that covers production and a fixed service charge that covers the plant's capital cost, which keeps low-volume households from subsidising large landscapers.

Water quality and health

Desalinated water is almost distilled in its mineral content, which raises two practical questions. First, it can be corrosive to distribution pipes because it has little buffering capacity; utilities stabilise it by adding calcium hydroxide or carbon dioxide before it leaves the plant, a step called remineralisation. Second, very soft water has been associated with lower intakes of calcium and magnesium, though the health evidence is observational and contested.

Disinfection by-products form when chlorine reacts with the small amount of natural organic matter that survives reverse osmosis. Because product water is low in organic carbon, plants typically maintain a free chlorine residual through the network rather than relying on chloramination, which can decay in dead ends. Regulatory frameworks in the European Union and the United States set separate limits for boron and bromate, both of which are concentrated in the feed and only partially rejected by the membrane.

Boron is the ion that most often forces a second pass. It exists as uncharged boric acid at seawater pH, so a single reverse osmosis stage rejects only about 70 to 85 percent of it. Raising the pH to 9 or 10 converts boron to the borate ion, which the membrane rejects far better, but the second pass then needs its own pressure and its own energy recovery loop. Plants serving irrigation in boron-sensitive crops, such as citrus and stone fruit, are the ones that routinely pay for that second pass.

Nitrates and pharmaceutical residues in brackish feed are a different problem again. Reverse osmosis rejects both well, but the concentrate carries them into the outfall, so a plant that cleans a river for drinking can still return micropollutants to the same river downstream. Advanced oxidation before the membrane or activated carbon after it are the usual answers, and both add a chemical cost line that rarely appears in the headline price per cubic metre.

Small-scale and emergency systems

Containerised reverse osmosis units have become the standard response after hurricanes and earthquakes. A 40-foot container can hold a complete train, including pretreatment, membrane racks, a diesel generator and a chlorination skid, and can be on a barge within seventy-two hours of a request. The limitation is feed water: after a storm the intake is often turbid with sediment, and without a settled basin the membranes blind within hours.

Solar-driven stills remain common on small islands and aboard fishing vessels. A parabolic trough can drive a single-effect still at roughly one litre per square metre per hour of sunshine, which is slow but needs no fuel and no spare parts beyond a wiper. NGOs distributing these units report high acceptance when the daily yield matches cooking demand, and abandonment when the unit is expected to fill a family's entire water need.

Household-scale electrodialysis reversal units treat brackish well water at villages too small for a piped scheme. They run at 12 or 24 volts, can be charged from a small solar array, and produce 200 to 500 litres a day. The membranes last two to four years in hard feed water, and the recurring replacement cost, not the capital cost, decides whether a village keeps the unit running.

Emergency procurement exposes the gap between nameplate capacity and delivered water. A donated 10,000 cubic metre per day plant is worthless without fuel, spare membranes, a trained operator and an outfall permit; several post-disaster reviews have found containers sitting unopened because the receiving country could not supply the last two. Capacity pledges that do not include an operating budget are closer to public relations than to water supply.

Future directions

Three research threads are likely to matter over the next decade. The first is brine valorisation: extracting magnesium, lithium and calcium from concentrate so that the outfall becomes a mineral product. Pilot crystallisers already recover magnesium hydroxide at grades that sell into flame-retardant and wastewater markets, though lithium recovery from seawater remains far below the concentration needed for economic extraction.

The second is low-energy membranes, including biomimetic aquaporin channels and graphene oxide laminates. Aquaporin-based hollow fibres have reached pilot scale in Denmark and show salt rejection above 99.5 percent at pressures close to osmotic, but manufacturing reproducibility and chlorine tolerance are unresolved.

The third is demand-side integration. Plants that can ramp with solar output, charge raised reservoirs during the day and draw them at night turn desalination from a constant load into a grid flexibility asset. Western Australia's Perth plants already follow that pattern with dedicated wind and solar farms, and the model is spreading to Chile and Namibia.

None of these directions removes the fundamental trade: freshwater from seawater is bought with energy, and the only durable way to lower the price is to lower the energy per cubic metre or to waste less of the water once it reaches the tap. Both levers are cheaper than building another plant.
